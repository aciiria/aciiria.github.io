During my internship at Tooth Matters, I helped with several stages of scaling and polishing, from preparing materials to assisting the dentist during treatment. I learned to coordinate my movements with the dentist's work, and I loved noticing how the procedure was adapted to different patients.

## Assisting During Scaling and Polishing

Scaling removes plaque and calculus, which forms when plaque hardens on the teeth. During this stage, I held the suction and high-vac (high-volume evacuator, or HVE) to remove saliva, water and debris, and I had to keep adjusting its position depending on where the dentist was working. When cleaning the outer surfaces of the teeth, I positioned the HVE inside the mouth to help protect the tongue. When cleaning the inner surfaces, I positioned it outside to retract the lips and keep the teeth accessible.

After scaling, I passed the dentist the polishing paste I had prepared and used the high-vac to remove excess paste and fluid. Polishing removes surface stains and residual deposits, so it complements scaling by tackling a different part of cleaning.

I also assisted during Prophy-Jet cleaning. I prepared the equipment, placed absorbent paper around the patient's mouth to catch splashes, and used the suction and high-vac during the procedure. This was the trickiest stage when I first started. If I held the high-vac at the wrong angle, water would splash everywhere. I had to learn to adjust its position to the direction of the spray while keeping the area clear for the dentist.

Afterwards, while the patient rinsed, I wiped down the Prophy-Jet equipment. Then I passed the dentist the fluoride gel I had prepared beforehand. Unlike scaling and polishing, which remove deposits and surface stains, fluoride strengthens enamel and supports its remineralisation, making teeth more resistant to decay.

## Adapting to Different Patients

One of my favourite things to watch was how the dentist adjusted both his explanations and his approach to the patient in front of him. With children, he would ask which show they wanted to watch on the television screen above the dental chair. He also explained unfamiliar instruments with comparisons they could relate to. Before inserting the saliva ejector, he described it as a straw that sucks up saliva; he compared polishing paste to toothpaste and polishing to brushing their teeth.

I thought this was a clever way to connect an unfamiliar procedure to things children already knew. Rather than expecting them to understand dental terminology, he made each step easier to anticipate. I also noticed that very young children were sometimes given water instead of mouthwash for the first rinse.

The instruments could vary too. For some very young children and elderly patients, the dentist used a manual sickle scaler instead of a powered scaler. This showed me that the same goal, removing deposits from the teeth, doesn't always call for the same approach. The choice of instrument depended on the patient and on the dentist's judgement.

## Reflection

Assisting with scaling and polishing made me much more aware of the coordination behind a dental procedure. Even positioning the high-vac properly meant paying attention to where the dentist was working and adjusting my movements to match. I also learned to anticipate the next stage of treatment and prepare materials while staying focused on the procedure.

At the same time, I saw that good care is more than following a sequence of steps. The dentist had to think about the technical requirements of the procedure and the needs of the patient in front of him. What I took from this is how those two things work together: the procedure has to be done well, but the approach also has to suit the individual.
