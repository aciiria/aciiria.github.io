A tooth can look completely ordinary from the outside, and an X-ray can still reveal something you'd never spot by looking into the mouth. During my internship at Tooth Matters, I learned how dentists use radiographs to examine teeth and the structures around them, and how a standardised numbering system lets them record exactly which tooth they're treating.

## Learning to Read Dental X-Rays

I was introduced to two types of dental radiographs: periapical (PA) and panoramic (OPG). A PA radiograph focuses on an individual tooth and the area around its root, while an OPG provides a broader view of the teeth and jaws.

These images can reveal problems that are difficult to spot during a visual examination. For example, dental decay may appear as a darker area on an X-ray because the affected tissue allows more X-rays to pass through it. Dark areas do not always indicate decay, however, and have to be interpreted alongside the other findings.

Radiographs can also show the pulp chamber and root canals, which contain the nerves and blood vessels inside a tooth. Changes around the tip of a root may indicate disease affecting the surrounding tissues. An OPG can also show the position of wisdom teeth, including whether they are impacted or angled towards neighbouring teeth.

I began to appreciate how much of a tooth's condition cannot be judged from its visible surface alone. The roots, surrounding bone and position of a tooth can all be relevant to understanding a patient's dental condition, even though these structures cannot be examined directly just by looking into the mouth.

## Using X-Rays to Explain Findings to Patients

I really enjoyed watching how the dentist displayed X-ray images on the screen in front of the dental chair and used them to explain findings to patients. Rather than describing a problem only in words, he could point to a specific area and show the patient exactly what was being discussed.

This showed me that radiographs do more than help dentists examine teeth. They also make an otherwise invisible problem easier for patients to understand. An X-ray gives the dentist a visual reference for explaining findings and discussing possible treatment.

## Identifying Teeth with the FDI Notation System

I was also introduced to the FDI tooth notation system, which assigns a tooth a two-digit number according to its quadrant and position from the midline. For example, tooth 11 is the upper right central incisor, while tooth 36 is the lower left first molar.

Letters can be added to specify the surfaces involved in treatment: M for mesial (facing the midline), D for distal (facing away from the midline), O for occlusal (the chewing surface of back teeth), B for buccal (facing the cheek), L for lingual (facing the tongue), and I for incisal (the biting edge of front teeth). For example, MO indicates the mesial and occlusal surfaces.

The dentist explained that recording the correct tooth and surface matters for both clinical documentation and administrative purposes, including subsidy claims and audits. A record needs to specify what treatment was performed and where, rather than simply stating that a patient received a filling.

The notation also provides a common language for communicating between practitioners. If a patient is referred to another dentist, precise records help identify the tooth involved and clarify the treatment history. When a crown is made by a dental laboratory, the correct tooth must likewise be identified alongside the scans or impressions and other specifications needed to produce the restoration.

## Reflection

Before my internship, I had not thought much about how information about a patient's teeth was communicated beyond the examination itself. Watching the dentist explain X-rays to patients and learning how tooth notation was used in records showed me two sides of the same process: helping patients understand their treatment, and ensuring that the people involved in their care have accurate information to work with.

I came away seeing communication as part of the clinical process itself. Identifying a problem matters, but so does explaining it clearly to the patient and recording it precisely enough for others to act on that information.
